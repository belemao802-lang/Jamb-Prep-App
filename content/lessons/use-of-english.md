# Use of English — Topic-by-Topic Lessons
**Content type:** Original learning material  
**Status:** Initial lesson pack; check topic/objective wording against the current official JAMB syllabus before release.

## 1. Comprehension and Summary

### Learning objectives
- Identify the main idea, supporting details, purpose, tone and attitude in a passage.
- Distinguish facts from opinions and inference from information stated directly.
- Summarise a passage without changing its meaning.

### Lesson
Read the questions first when this helps you know what information to look for, then read the passage carefully. The **main idea** is the central point; supporting details explain or prove it. An **inference** is a conclusion supported by clues in the text, not a guess based on personal opinion. A writer's **tone** may be critical, humorous, persuasive, anxious or objective. Choose the tone that the words and context actually support.

For summary questions, identify only the points requested. Combine related ideas, use your own concise wording, and avoid examples or repetition unless essential. Do not add opinions or facts that are absent from the passage.

### Subtopics
- Main idea and topic sentences
- Explicit details and evidence
- Inference and implication
- Writer's purpose, tone and attitude
- Vocabulary in context
- Summary points, concision and paraphrase

### Quick revision
Ask: What is the passage mainly about? Which sentence supports my answer? Is my conclusion supported by the text? Have I added anything the writer did not say?

## 2. Lexis and Structure

### Learning objectives
- Select words and grammatical structures that fit meaning and context.
- Recognise collocations, register, word forms and common usage patterns.
- Use surrounding words to infer an unfamiliar word's meaning.

### Lesson
**Lexis** concerns vocabulary and word choice; **structure** concerns how words and clauses are arranged. A word may be grammatically possible but unsuitable for the context. For example, English normally says “make a decision”, not “do a decision”; this is a **collocation**. Check both meaning and grammar when choosing an answer.

Word forms can change a sentence's function: *decide* (verb), *decision* (noun), *decisive* (adjective), *decisively* (adverb). Register matters too: formal writing may prefer “assist” where casual speech uses “help”. Use context rather than memorising isolated definitions.

### Subtopics
- Word meaning in context
- Collocations and idiomatic usage
- Word formation and parts of speech
- Formal, informal and technical register
- Prefixes, suffixes and roots
- Sentence completion

### Quick revision
Read the complete sentence with each option inserted. Check meaning, grammar, collocation and register.

## 3. Antonyms and Synonyms

### Learning objectives
- Choose a word with a similar meaning or an opposite meaning.
- Use context to select the correct sense of a word.
- Recognise that near-synonyms may differ in strength or usage.

### Lesson
A **synonym** has a similar meaning; an **antonym** has an opposite meaning. Exact equivalence is uncommon: *slim* and *thin* are similar, but their tone can differ. A word can also have several senses. *Bright* can mean giving much light, intelligent, or vivid. The sentence determines which sense is relevant.

For an antonym question, identify the meaning used in the sentence before selecting the opposite. Avoid choosing a word that is merely associated with the target word.

### Subtopics
- Contextual synonyms
- Contextual antonyms
- Shades of meaning
- Positive, neutral and negative connotations
- Multiple meanings and polysemy

### Quick revision
Define the target word as used in the question, then test each option against that definition.

## 4. Sentence Interpretation

### Learning objectives
- Interpret sentences that express implied meanings.
- Recognise paraphrase, emphasis, contrast and logical relationships.
- Avoid interpretations that exaggerate or reverse the original meaning.

### Lesson
Sentence interpretation tests what a statement means in context, not just the literal meaning of each word. A phrase such as “She barely passed” means she passed, but only by a small margin. “Unless you hurry, you will miss the bus” means that failing to hurry will result in missing it.

When selecting a paraphrase, preserve the original meaning, time, degree and logical relationship. Be careful with negatives, conditionals, comparisons and words such as *only*, *hardly*, *unless* and *despite*.

### Subtopics
- Paraphrase and restatement
- Implied meaning
- Condition and consequence
- Contrast and concession
- Degree and emphasis
- Negation and logical meaning

### Quick revision
Check whether the option preserves who did what, when it happened, and the strength or condition of the statement.

## 5. Idioms and Phrasal Verbs

### Learning objectives
- Interpret common idioms as expressions with established meanings.
- Understand phrasal verbs formed from a verb and a particle.
- Use idioms and phrasal verbs appropriately in context.

### Lesson
An **idiom** is an expression whose meaning cannot always be worked out literally. “Break the ice” means to make people feel more comfortable at the start of an interaction. A **phrasal verb** combines a verb with an adverb or preposition, often changing the verb's meaning: *give up* means stop trying; *look after* means take care of.

Some phrasal verbs are separable (*turn the light off / turn off the light*), while others are not (*look after the child*, not normally *look the child after*). Pronouns usually go between the verb and particle in separable phrasal verbs: *turn it off*.

### Subtopics
- Common idioms and their meanings
- Phrasal verbs and particles
- Separable and inseparable phrasal verbs
- Literal versus figurative meaning
- Context-appropriate usage

### Quick revision
Learn each expression with a short example sentence rather than memorising a word-for-word translation.

## 6. Parts of Speech

### Learning objectives
- Identify the grammatical function of words in sentences.
- Distinguish nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs, prepositions, conjunctions and interjections.
- Recognise that one word can belong to different classes in different sentences.

### Lesson
A **noun** names a person, place, thing or idea; a **pronoun** stands in for a noun phrase; a **verb** expresses an action, event or state. An **adjective** modifies a noun, while an **adverb** modifies a verb, adjective, adverb or sometimes a whole clause. A **preposition** shows a relationship, often of time, place or direction. A **conjunction** joins words, phrases or clauses.

Class depends on use. In “I drink water” *drink* is a verb; in “Have a drink” it is a noun. Identify what the word does in the sentence, not only its spelling.

### Subtopics
- Nouns and noun phrases
- Pronouns and reference
- Main, auxiliary and modal verbs
- Adjectives and determiners
- Adverbs
- Prepositions and prepositional phrases
- Coordinating and subordinating conjunctions
- Interjections

### Quick revision
Name the word class, then explain the word's function in the particular sentence.

## 7. Concord and Agreement

### Learning objectives
- Match subjects with appropriate verb forms.
- Apply agreement rules to collective nouns, indefinite pronouns and coordinated subjects.
- Avoid errors caused by words placed between the subject and verb.

### Lesson
**Concord** means grammatical agreement. A singular subject normally takes a singular verb in the present tense: “The student writes.” A plural subject takes a plural verb: “The students write.” Do not let a nearby noun distract you: “The box of books **is** heavy” because the subject is *box*, not *books*.

With *neither…nor* and *either…or*, the verb commonly agrees with the nearer subject in standard exam usage. Indefinite pronouns such as *everyone*, *someone* and *each* are grammatically singular: “Everyone is ready.” Collective nouns may take singular or plural agreement depending on whether the group is treated as one unit or as individuals; follow the sentence meaning and the convention expected in the question.

### Subtopics
- Subject–verb agreement
- Singular and plural subjects
- Subjects separated from verbs
- Indefinite pronouns
- Either/or and neither/nor
- Collective nouns
- Agreement with quantities and measurements

### Quick revision
Find the true subject first, determine whether it is singular or plural, then select the matching verb.

## 8. Tenses and Sequence of Tenses

### Learning objectives
- Use simple, continuous, perfect and perfect-continuous forms.
- Match tense to time, duration, completion and relevance.
- Maintain logical tense relationships in reported speech and complex sentences.

### Lesson
Tense and aspect help show when an action occurs and how it unfolds. The **present simple** describes habits and general truths (“Water boils at 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure”). The **present continuous** describes an action in progress or a temporary situation. The **present perfect** connects a past event with the present; the **past perfect** marks an event earlier than another past event.

Time expressions provide clues: *since* often marks a starting point; *for* often marks a duration. Do not choose tense by a single word alone—read the whole sentence and its time relationship. In reported speech, tense may shift when the reporting verb is in the past, but facts that remain generally true may retain the present tense.

### Subtopics
- Present, past and future forms
- Simple and continuous aspect
- Perfect and perfect-continuous aspect
- Time expressions: since, for, already, yet
- Sequence of tenses
- Conditional sentences
- Reported speech

### Quick revision
Mark the time of each action and decide whether it is habitual, ongoing, completed or earlier than another event.

## 9. Question Tags

### Learning objectives
- Form question tags using the correct auxiliary and pronoun.
- Apply positive-statement/negative-tag and negative-statement/positive-tag patterns.
- Recognise special forms such as *I am*, imperatives and *let's*.

### Lesson
A positive statement usually takes a negative tag: “She is ready, **isn't she?**” A negative statement usually takes a positive tag: “They haven't left, **have they?**” Repeat the auxiliary or modal verb from the statement; if there is no auxiliary in the present or past simple, use *do/does/did*.

Use a pronoun in the tag, not the full noun. Common special forms include “I'm late, **aren't I?**” and “Let's begin, **shall we?**” Intonation can make a tag a genuine question or a request for confirmation, but grammar questions usually focus on the correct form.

### Subtopics
- Auxiliary and modal verbs in tags
- Positive and negative polarity
- Pronoun selection
- Tags with simple tenses
- Special forms: I am, let's, imperatives

### Quick revision
Check statement polarity, identify the auxiliary, and replace the subject with the correct pronoun.

## 10. Punctuation and Spelling

### Learning objectives
- Use full stops, commas, semicolons, colons, apostrophes and quotation marks correctly.
- Distinguish possessives from contractions.
- Recognise commonly confused spellings and punctuation that changes meaning.

### Lesson
Punctuation helps readers understand sentence structure. Use a full stop to end a statement; a comma to separate items or mark certain introductory and non-essential elements; a semicolon to connect closely related independent clauses; and a colon to introduce an explanation or list. Do not join two complete sentences with only a comma.

An apostrophe marks omission (*don't*) or possession (*the student's book*). Plural nouns do not automatically take apostrophes. Compare *its* (possessive) with *it's* (it is/it has), and *their*, *there* and *they're*. Spelling questions often test words that sound alike but have different meanings.

### Subtopics
- Sentence-ending punctuation
- Commas and lists
- Semicolons and colons
- Apostrophes and possession
- Quotation marks
- Capitalisation
- Commonly confused words and spelling patterns

### Quick revision
Read the sentence aloud, identify its clauses, and check whether each punctuation mark has a clear grammatical purpose.

## 11. Register and Word Choice

### Learning objectives
- Select vocabulary suitable for a particular profession, setting or communicative purpose.
- Recognise formal, informal, technical and specialised language.
- Use context to identify the intended register.

### Lesson
**Register** is the style of language suited to a situation, audience or field. A medical report may use *diagnosis* and *symptoms*; a casual conversation may use simpler wording. A formal letter usually avoids slang and overly casual contractions. Register questions often describe a setting—such as a court, hospital, classroom, market or newsroom—and ask for the word most appropriate to it.

The best answer is not always the longest or most technical word. Choose the word that fits both the meaning and the situation.

### Subtopics
- Formal and informal language
- Professional and technical vocabulary
- Language of public institutions
- Audience and purpose
- Appropriate and inappropriate word choice

### Quick revision
Identify who is speaking, to whom, for what purpose and in what setting before choosing the expression.

## 12. Oral English: Vowels

### Learning objectives
- Distinguish vowel sounds from vowel letters.
- Recognise common monophthongs and diphthongs in phonetic notation.
- Compare vowel sounds by tongue position, openness and lip shape.

### Lesson
A vowel **letter** is a written symbol; a vowel **sound** is what is pronounced. English spelling does not reliably predict pronunciation, so phonetic symbols are useful. A **monophthong** is a vowel sound produced with relatively stable tongue position; a **diphthong** involves movement from one vowel quality toward another within one syllable.

Vowels can differ in tongue height, frontness/backness, lip rounding and length. When answering sound questions, compare pronunciation rather than spelling. Accents vary, so follow the pronunciation model and phonetic symbols specified by the syllabus or question.

### Subtopics
- Vowel letters and vowel phonemes
- Short and long vowel contrasts
- Monophthongs
- Diphthongs
- IPA symbols and minimal pairs
- Stress and vowel reduction

### Quick revision
Say the words aloud, isolate the target vowel sound, and compare it with the options.

## 13. Oral English: Consonants

### Learning objectives
- Classify consonants by place and manner of articulation and voicing.
- Distinguish consonant phonemes that may be represented by similar spellings.
- Recognise minimal pairs and common pronunciation contrasts.

### Lesson
Consonants are described by **place of articulation** (where airflow is narrowed or stopped), **manner of articulation** (how airflow is affected) and **voicing** (whether the vocal folds vibrate). For example, /p/ and /b/ are both bilabial plosives, but /p/ is voiceless and /b/ is voiced.

Letters and sounds do not always correspond one-to-one. The letter combination *sh* represents one sound in *ship*, while the letter *x* commonly represents two sounds /ks/ in *box*. Use phonetic symbols and pronunciation, not spelling alone.

### Subtopics
- Place of articulation
- Manner of articulation
- Voiced and voiceless consonants
- Plosives, fricatives, affricates, nasals and approximants
- Minimal pairs
- Consonant clusters and silent letters

### Quick revision
For a pair of sounds, compare voicing, place and manner; identify exactly which feature changes.

## 14. Stress and Intonation

### Learning objectives
- Identify syllable stress in words.
- Recognise how stress can distinguish word classes or meanings.
- Interpret common intonation patterns in questions, statements and emphasis.

### Lesson
**Word stress** is the prominence given to one syllable in a word. Stress can change meaning or grammatical class: *REcord* is commonly a noun, while *reCORD* is commonly a verb. **Sentence stress** highlights important information. **Intonation** is the pattern of pitch movement across an utterance; it can signal completion, a question, uncertainty, contrast or emotion.

Do not assume every question ends with rising intonation. Yes/no questions often rise, while many wh-questions fall, though context and speaker intention affect the pattern. In exam questions, pay attention to the specific word or syllable marked.

### Subtopics
- Syllables and word stress
- Stress shift in related words
- Sentence stress and emphasis
- Falling and rising intonation
- Contrastive stress
- Intonation in questions and statements

### Quick revision
Mark the stressed syllable, then consider whether the pitch pattern changes the speaker's intention.

## 15. Cloze Passages

### Learning objectives
- Use grammar, vocabulary, collocation and overall meaning to complete gaps.
- Track references and logical links across sentences.
- Select the option that fits the passage as a whole.

### Lesson
A **cloze passage** removes words from a connected text. Each gap should be solved using both local grammar and the passage's overall meaning. First read the entire passage to understand the subject and tone. Then check the words immediately before and after the gap, the required part of speech, tense, agreement, collocation and logical connectors.

After selecting an option, read the full sentence and passage again. A word can fit grammatically but break the meaning or connection between sentences.

### Subtopics
- Grammar-based gaps
- Vocabulary and collocation
- Connectors and transition words
- Pronoun reference
- Tense and agreement
- Whole-passage coherence

### Quick revision
Use three checks for each gap: grammar, meaning and connection to the surrounding text.
