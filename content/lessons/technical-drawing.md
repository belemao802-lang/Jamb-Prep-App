# Technical Drawing — Topic-by-Topic Lessons
**Content type:** Original learning material. **Status:** Starter teaching pack; verify topic coverage against the current official JAMB syllabus and your prescribed drawing conventions.

## 1. Drawing Instruments and Conventions
Common instruments include drawing board, T-square, set squares, scale rule, compass, divider, protractor, pencils and eraser. Use sharp pencils, clean paper and light construction lines. Line types communicate meaning: visible outlines are thick continuous lines; hidden edges are dashed; centre lines use a chain pattern. Follow the conventions specified in the question.

## 2. Geometrical Construction
Geometrical construction uses instruments to create accurate lines, angles, perpendiculars, bisectors and shapes. A perpendicular bisector divides a line segment into two equal parts at 90°. An angle bisector divides an angle into two equal angles. Leave construction arcs visible when required to show the method.

## 3. Angles, Polygons and Circles
A polygon is a closed plane figure made from straight segments. The sum of interior angles of an n-sided polygon is (n−2)×180°. A regular polygon has equal sides and equal angles. A circle is defined by points at a constant distance from its centre; diameter=2×radius.

## 4. Loci
A locus is the path traced by a point satisfying a condition. Points at a fixed distance from one point form a circle. Points equidistant from two fixed points lie on the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them. Points equidistant from two intersecting lines lie on their angle bisectors.

## 5. Scales and Dimensioning
A scale relates drawing size to actual size. A 1:100 scale means 1 unit on the drawing represents 100 of the same units in reality. Dimension lines, extension lines, arrowheads and figures should be clear and not ambiguous. Convert units consistently before measuring or calculating.

## 6. Orthographic Projection
Orthographic projection represents a 3D object through 2D views projected perpendicular to projection planes. Front, plan (top) and end views show different dimensions. Align views correctly and project features between views. Hidden details may be shown with dashed lines when needed.

## 7. Isometric and Oblique Drawing
An isometric drawing shows three principal axes equally spaced, conventionally with two receding axes at 30° to the horizontal and vertical edges vertical. In oblique drawing, the front face is drawn in true shape while receding depth lines follow the chosen oblique angle and scale. Do not confuse pictorial views with orthographic views.

## 8. First-Angle and Third-Angle Projection
These are systems for arranging orthographic views. In first-angle projection, the object is between the observer and the projection plane; the plan is placed below the front view and the right-side view appears to the left. In third-angle projection, the projection plane is between observer and object; the plan is above the front view and the right-side view appears to the right. Always check the projection symbol on the drawing.

## 9. Sectional Views
A section reveals internal details by imagining the object cut by a plane. The cut surfaces are shown with section lining (hatching), usually evenly spaced. Do not hatch empty spaces or separate parts that are not cut. A cutting-plane line indicates where the imaginary cut is made.

## 10. Development of Surfaces
Development unfolds a 3D surface into a flat pattern. Prisms and cylinders are commonly developed by laying out their side faces or circumference and height. For a cylinder, the lateral development is a rectangle whose length is the circumference πd and whose width is the cylinder height. Add allowances only when the question specifies them.

## 11. Intersections of Solids
Intersection drawings show where two solids meet or pass through one another. Find key points where edges or surface generators intersect, transfer those points between views, then join them in the correct order. Visible portions use continuous lines; hidden portions use dashed lines where appropriate.

## 12. Auxiliary Projection
Auxiliary views reveal the true shape or size of an inclined surface that appears foreshortened in the principal views. Project perpendicular to the inclined surface onto an auxiliary plane, transferring distances carefully from the related view.

## 13. Assembly and Detail Drawing
A detail drawing gives enough information to manufacture one component, including shape, dimensions, material and tolerances where required. An assembly drawing shows how components fit together and may include item numbers and a parts list. Avoid duplicating dimensions or leaving critical sizes unspecified.

## 14. Building Drawing
Building drawings can include plans, elevations, sections and site plans. A floor plan is a horizontal view showing room layout and wall positions. An elevation shows the external face from a chosen direction. Read scales, symbols, dimensions and north arrows carefully; local standards may vary.

## 15. Electrical and Mechanical Symbols
Standard symbols represent components more clearly than lengthy labels. Use the symbol legend provided and do not invent symbols. Mechanical drawings may include fasteners, bearings and joints; electrical drawings may include switches, lamps, sockets and protective devices. Symbols and conventions must be consistent throughout a drawing.

## 16. Freehand Sketching
Freehand sketches communicate an idea without relying on drawing instruments for every line. Observe proportions, main shapes, alignment and important features first. Use light guidelines, then strengthen final outlines. A sketch should be clear and correctly proportioned even if it is not perfectly scaled.

## 17. Working Drawings and Interpretation
A working drawing communicates the information needed to make or build an object. Read title block, scale, projection symbol, line types, dimensions and notes before interpreting it. Check that views agree and that units are consistent. Never infer a missing dimension unless the question gives enough information.

## Quick Revision Checklist
- Learn the purpose of each instrument and common line types.
- Practise first-angle and third-angle view arrangement.
- Convert drawing measurements using the stated scale.
- Distinguish orthographic, isometric and oblique drawings.
- Apply section lining, development and dimensioning conventions consistently.
- Use the projection symbol and any supplied legend rather than guessing.
