## 1. Purpose of Accounting

Accounting identifies, records, classifies and summarises financial transactions so users can make decisions. Bookkeeping is the systematic recording of transactions; accounting also interprets and communicates financial information.

**Revision focus:** Define the key terms, explain the main principle in your own words, and apply it to an example or a JAMB-style question.

## 2. Accounting Equation

The basic accounting equation is Assets = Capital + Liabilities. Assets are resources controlled by a business, liabilities are obligations to outsiders, and capital represents the owner's residual interest. Every transaction must preserve the equation.

## 3. Double-Entry Bookkeeping

Each transaction affects at least two accounts, with total debits equal to total credits. Debit and credit mean the left and right sides of an account; they do not automatically mean increase and decrease for every account type.

**Revision focus:** Define the key terms, explain the main principle in your own words, and apply it to an example or a JAMB-style question.

## 4. Source Documents and Books of Original Entry

Invoices, receipts, credit notes, debit notes and payment vouchers provide evidence of transactions. Transactions are first recorded in suitable books of original entry, such as sales journals, purchases journals, cash books or general journals.

**Revision focus:** Define the key terms, explain the main principle in your own words, and apply it to an example or a JAMB-style question.

## 5. Ledger and Trial Balance

Ledger accounts group transactions by account. Balancing an account determines its net balance. A trial balance lists ledger balances to test arithmetic equality of debits and credits, but it does not reveal every error.

**Revision focus:** Define the key terms, explain the main principle in your own words, and apply it to an example or a JAMB-style question.

## 6. Cash Book and Bank Reconciliation

A cash book records cash and bank transactions. A bank reconciliation explains differences between the cash book and bank statement, such as unpresented cheques, deposits not yet credited and bank charges not yet recorded.

## 7. Trading, Profit and Loss Accounts

A trading account calculates gross profit: sales less cost of goods sold. A profit and loss account accounts for expenses and other income to calculate net profit or loss. Distinguish gross profit from net profit.

## 8. Balance Sheet and Depreciation

A statement of financial position reports assets, liabilities and capital at a date. Depreciation allocates the depreciable amount of a non-current asset over its useful life; it is not simply a cash fund set aside for replacement.
