# Biology — Topic-by-Topic Lessons
**Use:** JAMB UTME revision. These are original study notes, not a substitute for the current official syllabus or a full textbook.

## 1. Characteristics and Classification of Living Things
**Subtopics:** nutrition, respiration, movement, growth, excretion, reproduction, sensitivity; classification; binomial nomenclature.
Living things carry out life processes. Respiration releases usable energy from food; it is not the same as breathing. Excretion removes metabolic waste, while egestion removes undigested food. Classification groups organisms by shared characteristics. In binomial nomenclature, the genus begins with a capital letter and the species name with a lowercase letter; both are italicised when typed.
**Check yourself:** Explain the difference between excretion and egestion; state the purpose of classification.

## 2. The Cell
**Subtopics:** cell theory, plant and animal cells, organelles, prokaryotes and eukaryotes, specialised cells.
The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. The nucleus contains genetic material; the cell membrane controls movement in and out; mitochondria release energy during aerobic respiration; ribosomes make proteins. Plant cells usually have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts in photosynthetic tissues and a large permanent vacuole. Bacteria are prokaryotic and lack a membrane-bound nucleus.
**Check yourself:** Compare plant and animal cells; link an organelle to its function.

## 3. Levels of Organisation
**Subtopics:** cell, tissue, organ, system, organism; examples in plants and animals.
Similar cells form tissues; tissues combine to form organs; organs work together in systems. Examples include muscle tissue, the heart and the circulatory system. In plants, xylem and phloem are transport tissues.
**Check yourself:** Arrange the levels from simplest to most complex.

## 4. Cell Division
**Subtopics:** interphase, mitosis, meiosis, chromosome number, importance.
Mitosis produces two genetically similar daughter cells and supports growth, repair and asexual reproduction. Meiosis produces haploid cells for sexual reproduction and introduces variation. DNA is replicated before division during interphase; interphase is not itself a stage of mitosis.
**Check yourself:** Why must gametes be haploid?

## 5. Nutrition in Plants
**Subtopics:** photosynthesis, chlorophyll, light and dark-dependent reactions, limiting factors, mineral nutrition.
Photosynthesis uses light energy to make carbohydrates from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen. Chlorophyll absorbs light. Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature can limit the rate. Nitrates are needed to make amino acids and proteins; magnesium is needed for chlorophyll.
**Check yourself:** Write the word equation for photosynthesis and name two limiting factors.

## 6. Nutrition in Animals
**Subtopics:** food classes, balanced diet, digestion, enzymes, absorption and assimilation.
Carbohydrates supply energy, proteins support growth and repair, fats store energy and insulate, while vitamins, minerals, fibre and water have essential supporting roles. Amylase digests starch; proteases digest proteins; lipases digest fats. Bile emulsifies fats and helps neutralise acidic chyme but is not an enzyme. Most absorption occurs in the small intestine through villi.
**Check yourself:** Match digestive enzymes to their substrates.

## 7. Transport in Plants
**Subtopics:** xylem, phloem, root hairs, transpiration, translocation.
Xylem transports water and mineral salts mainly upward and also supports the plant. Phloem translocates sucrose and other organic solutes from sources to sinks. Root hairs increase absorptive surface area. Transpiration is water-vapour loss from aerial plant parts, chiefly through stomata; it helps pull water through the xylem.
**Check yourself:** Distinguish transpiration from translocation.

## 8. Transport in Animals
**Subtopics:** blood components, heart, vessels, circulation, lymph.
Plasma transports dissolved substances; red blood cells carry oxygen using haemoglobin; white blood cells defend against pathogens; platelets aid clotting. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return blood to it, and capillaries permit exchange. Humans have double circulation: pulmonary and systemic.
**Check yourself:** Compare arteries, veins and capillaries.

## 9. Respiration
**Subtopics:** aerobic and anaerobic respiration, gas exchange, respiratory organs.
Aerobic respiration releases energy from glucose using oxygen and produces carbon dioxide and water. Anaerobic respiration releases less energy without oxygen; in human muscles it produces lactate, while yeast produces ethanol and carbon dioxide. Gas exchange surfaces are thin, moist and have a large surface area.
**Check yourself:** Compare the products of anaerobic respiration in muscles and yeast.

## 10. Excretion and Homeostasis
**Subtopics:** kidney, nephron, liver, lungs, skin, water and temperature regulation.
Excretion removes metabolic waste. Kidneys filter blood, selectively reabsorb useful substances and regulate water and salts. The liver deaminates excess amino acids and converts toxic ammonia to urea. Homeostasis keeps internal conditions within suitable limits; hormones and negative feedback often regulate it.
**Check yourself:** Explain why urea is formed and how kidneys help regulate water balance.

## 11. Support and Movement
**Subtopics:** skeleton, joints, muscles, plant support, tropisms.
The skeleton supports and protects the body and provides attachment for muscles. At a movable joint, muscles often work in antagonistic pairs: one contracts while the other relaxes. Plants gain support from turgor and strengthening tissues. Tropisms are directional growth responses to stimuli such as light or gravity.
**Check yourself:** Explain how a biceps–triceps pair moves the forearm.

## 12. Coordination and Control
**Subtopics:** nervous system, reflex arc, sense organs, hormones, endocrine glands.
Neurons transmit impulses. A reflex arc commonly involves a receptor, sensory neuron, relay neuron, motor neuron and effector. Hormones are chemical messengers carried in blood to target cells. The pancreas produces insulin, which lowers blood glucose, and glucagon, which raises it.
**Check yourself:** Compare nervous and hormonal coordination.

## 13. Reproduction in Plants
**Subtopics:** flower parts, pollination, fertilisation, seed and fruit formation, germination, vegetative propagation.
The anther produces pollen and the stigma receives it. Pollination is pollen transfer from anther to stigma; fertilisation is fusion of gametes. After fertilisation, the ovule develops into a seed and the ovary usually develops into a fruit. Germination requires suitable water, oxygen and temperature.
**Check yourself:** Distinguish pollination from fertilisation.

## 14. Reproduction in Animals and Humans
**Subtopics:** male and female reproductive systems, menstrual cycle, fertilisation, pregnancy, birth control and reproductive health.
Sperm are produced in the testes; ova mature in the ovaries. Fertilisation normally occurs in the oviduct. The placenta allows exchange between maternal and foetal blood without normally mixing the two blood supplies directly. Reproductive health includes accurate information, hygiene and prevention of sexually transmitted infections.
**Check yourself:** State the functions of the placenta and uterus.

## 15. Growth and Development
**Subtopics:** growth measurement, development, metamorphosis, plant growth.
Growth involves a lasting increase in size or dry mass, often through cell division and enlargement. Development includes changes in form and function. Incomplete metamorphosis has egg, nymph and adult stages; complete metamorphosis has egg, larva, pupa and adult stages.
**Check yourself:** Compare complete and incomplete metamorphosis.

## 16. Genetics and Inheritance
**Subtopics:** genes, alleles, genotype, phenotype, dominant and recessive alleles, monohybrid crosses, sex determination.
Genes are DNA sections that influence inherited traits. Genotype is an organism's allele combination; phenotype is its observable characteristics influenced by genes and environment. For a simple cross Aa × Aa, the expected genotype ratio is 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa, assuming complete dominance and equal segregation.
**Check yourself:** Use a Punnett square to predict offspring genotypes.

## 17. Variation and Evolution
**Subtopics:** continuous and discontinuous variation, mutation, natural selection, adaptation, evidence for evolution.
Continuous variation shows a range, such as height; discontinuous variation has distinct categories, such as some blood groups. Mutation creates new genetic variants. Natural selection occurs when heritable traits that improve reproductive success become more common over generations. Individuals do not evolve intentionally in response to need.
**Check yourself:** Give examples of both types of variation and explain natural selection.

## 18. Ecology and Ecosystems
**Subtopics:** habitat, population, community, ecosystem, biotic and abiotic factors, food chains and webs.
A habitat is where an organism lives; a population is members of one species in an area; a community includes populations of different species. Ecosystems include organisms and their physical environment. Producers capture energy, consumers feed on organisms and decomposers break down dead material. Energy is lost between trophic levels, so food chains are usually short.
**Check yourself:** Distinguish habitat, population and community.

## 19. Energy Flow and Nutrient Cycles
**Subtopics:** food pyramids, energy transfer, carbon and nitrogen cycles, decomposers.
Energy flows through ecosystems and is not recycled; matter is recycled. Photosynthesis removes carbon dioxide from air, while respiration, decomposition and combustion return it. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert atmospheric nitrogen into usable compounds; decomposers return nitrogen compounds to soil.
**Check yourself:** Explain why energy decreases at successive trophic levels.

## 20. Population Studies and Adaptation
**Subtopics:** population size, density, competition, carrying capacity, predator–prey relationships, adaptations.
Population size changes with births, deaths, immigration and emigration. Competition increases when resources are limited. Carrying capacity is the population an environment can sustain under given conditions. Structural, behavioural and physiological adaptations improve survival or reproduction in a particular environment.
**Check yourself:** List four processes that change population size.

## 21. Microorganisms and Disease
**Subtopics:** bacteria, viruses, fungi, protozoa, pathogens, transmission, immunity and prevention.
Microorganisms include useful and harmful forms. Viruses replicate only inside host cells. Pathogens cause disease and may spread through air, water, food, body fluids or vectors. Hygiene, vaccination, safe water, sanitation and appropriate treatment help control infectious disease. Antibiotics act against susceptible bacteria, not viruses.
**Check yourself:** Explain why antibiotics do not cure viral infections.

## 22. Conservation of Natural Resources
**Subtopics:** biodiversity, pollution, deforestation, erosion, sustainable use, protected areas.
Biodiversity includes genetic, species and ecosystem diversity. Habitat destruction, pollution, overexploitation and invasive species can reduce it. Conservation includes habitat protection, restoration, sustainable harvesting, pollution control and environmental education.
**Check yourself:** Name human activities that threaten biodiversity and suggest remedies.

## 23. Soil and Water
**Subtopics:** soil profile, soil organisms, soil types, water cycle, soil fertility and erosion.
Soil contains mineral particles, organic matter, air and water. Humus improves structure and nutrient availability. Soil texture affects drainage and water retention. The water cycle includes evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation and runoff. Vegetation reduces erosion by protecting soil and slowing surface flow.
**Check yourself:** Explain two ways to maintain soil fertility.

## 24. Practical Biology and Biological Drawing
**Subtopics:** observation, magnification, drawing rules, tables, graphs, food tests and microscope use.
Scientific drawings should be large, clear, proportional and labelled with straight ruled lines; avoid shading unless instructed. Magnification = image size ÷ actual size, using the same units. Record observations before conclusions. Common food tests include iodine for starch, Biuret for protein and emulsion test for lipids.
**Check yourself:** Calculate magnification and state positive results for common food tests.

## 25. Agriculture and Applied Biology
**Subtopics:** crop and livestock improvement, pests, diseases, soil management, biotechnology and public health.
Biology is applied in breeding, pest management, food production, sanitation and conservation. Crop rotation can maintain soil fertility and interrupt pest cycles. Biological control uses living organisms to reduce pests, but ecological impacts should be considered before release.
**Check yourself:** Explain how crop rotation and biological control can support sustainable farming.
