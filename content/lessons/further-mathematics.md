# Further Mathematics — Topic-by-Topic Lessons
**Content type:** Original learning material. **Status:** Starter teaching pack; compare each topic and objective with the current official JAMB syllabus before treating this as complete.

## 1. Functions and Relations
A relation pairs members of one set with members of another. A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. The domain is the set of inputs; the range is the set of outputs actually produced. For example, if f(x)=2x+3, then f(4)=11.
**Remember:** Different inputs may have the same output, but one input cannot have two outputs in a function.

## 2. Polynomial Equations
A polynomial is an expression such as ax²+bx+c. Its degree is the highest power with a non-zero coefficient. To solve a polynomial equation, factor it where possible and use the zero-product rule. For x²−5x+6=0, factor to (x−2)(x−3)=0, so x=2 or x=3.
**Check:** Substitute each root into the original equation.

## 3. Complex Numbers
A complex number has the form a+bi, where i²=−1. The real part is a and the imaginary part is b. Add and subtract by collecting like parts. Multiply using expansion and replace i² with −1. The conjugate of a+bi is a−bi.
**Example:** (2+3i)+(4−i)=6+2i.

## 4. Mathematical Induction
Mathematical induction proves a statement for all positive integers in a sequence. First verify the base case, usually n=1. Then assume the statement is true for n=k and use that assumption to prove it for n=k+1. Both stages are essential.
**Common error:** Proving only the base case does not prove the whole statement.

## 5. Binomial Theorem
For a non-negative integer n, (a+b)^n can be expanded using binomial coefficients: (a+b)^n=Σ C(n,r)a^(n−r)b^r. The coefficients in row n of Pascal’s triangle give the same coefficients. The general term helps find a particular power without expanding every term.
**Example:** (x+2)²=x²+4x+4.

## 6. Permutations and Combinations
A permutation counts arrangements where order matters: nPr=n!/(n−r)!. A combination counts selections where order does not matter: nCr=n!/[r!(n−r)!]. Factorial means n!=n(n−1)…1, with 0!=1.
**Tip:** Seating people is usually an arrangement; choosing a committee is usually a selection.

## 7. Matrices and Determinants
A matrix is a rectangular arrangement of entries. Addition requires equal dimensions. Matrix multiplication is possible when the first matrix’s column count equals the second matrix’s row count. For a 2×2 matrix [[a,b],[c,d]], the determinant is ad−bc.
**Example:** det([[2,1],[3,4]])=8−3=5.

## 8. Vectors in Two and Three Dimensions
A vector has magnitude and direction and may be written in component form. Add vectors component by component. The magnitude of (a,b) is √(a²+b²); in three dimensions, the magnitude of (a,b,c) is √(a²+b²+c²). The dot product is a·b=a₁b₁+a₂b₂(+a₃b₃).
**Use:** Vectors describe displacement, velocity and forces.

## 9. Coordinate Geometry
The gradient of a line through (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂) is m=(y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁), when x₂≠x₁. A line can be written y−y₁=m(x−x₁). Parallel non-vertical lines have equal gradients; perpendicular non-vertical lines have gradients whose product is −1.
**Example:** The gradient between (1,2) and (3,8) is 3.

## 10. Conic Sections
Conic sections include circles, parabolas, ellipses and hyperbolas. A circle with centre (h,k) and radius r has equation (x−h)²+(y−k)²=r². A parabola can be described as points equidistant from a focus and a directrix. Identify a conic by its standard equation and key features.
**Study focus:** Centre, radius, focus, directrix, axes and vertices where applicable.

## 11. Sequences and Series
A sequence is an ordered list; a series is the sum of its terms. An arithmetic progression has common difference d and nth term aₙ=a+(n−1)d. Its first n terms sum to Sₙ=n/2[2a+(n−1)d]. A geometric progression has common ratio r and nth term ar^(n−1).
**Example:** In 3, 7, 11, … the common difference is 4.

## 12. Limits and Continuity
A limit describes the value a function approaches as x approaches a point. For polynomials, direct substitution often evaluates a limit. If substitution gives 0/0, factorise or simplify before substituting again. A function is continuous at a point when its value exists there and equals the limit from both sides.
**Caution:** A limit may exist even when the function is undefined at the point.

## 13. Differentiation
Differentiation gives the instantaneous rate of change or gradient of a curve. The power rule is d(xⁿ)/dx=nxⁿ⁻¹. Constants differentiate to zero, and constant multiples can be taken outside the derivative.
**Example:** d(3x²−4x+7)/dx=6x−4.

## 14. Applications of Differentiation
The derivative gives the gradient of a tangent. At a stationary point, dy/dx=0. The second derivative can help classify a stationary point: if y''>0, the curve has a local minimum; if y''<0, it has a local maximum, subject to the usual conditions. Derivatives also model velocity and acceleration from displacement.
**Method:** Differentiate, solve y'=0, then test the result and interpret the context.

## 15. Integration
Integration reverses differentiation and calculates accumulated quantities. The power rule is ∫xⁿ dx=xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)+C for n≠−1. The constant C is required for an indefinite integral. A definite integral evaluates an antiderivative at the upper limit minus its value at the lower limit.
**Example:** ∫2x dx=x²+C.

## 16. Differential Equations
A differential equation relates a function to one or more of its derivatives. A simple separable equation can often be solved by placing terms involving y on one side and x on the other, then integrating both sides. Use an initial condition to determine the integration constant.
**Check:** Differentiate your proposed solution and substitute it back into the equation.

## 17. Numerical Methods
Numerical methods approximate solutions when an exact algebraic solution is difficult. Iteration repeatedly applies a rule to improve an estimate. In interval methods such as bisection, choose an interval where a continuous function changes sign, then halve the interval repeatedly.
**Good practice:** Keep sufficient decimal places during working and round only at the end.

## 18. Probability Distributions
A random variable assigns numerical values to outcomes. For a discrete distribution, probabilities are non-negative and their total is 1. The expected value is E(X)=ΣxP(X=x). For a binomial distribution, P(X=r)=C(n,r)p^r(1−p)^(n−r), when trials are independent and have constant success probability p.
**Remember:** State the assumptions before applying a distribution formula.

## 19. Statistics and Correlation
The mean is the arithmetic average; the median is the middle value after ordering; the mode is the most frequent value. Variance measures spread around the mean and standard deviation is its square root. Correlation describes the strength and direction of a linear association, but correlation alone does not prove causation.
**Interpretation:** A correlation near +1 is strong positive linear association; near −1 is strong negative association.

## 20. Mechanics
Mechanics applies mathematics to motion and forces. For constant acceleration, v=u+at, s=ut+½at² and v²=u²+2as. Newton’s second law is F=ma. Weight is W=mg; momentum is p=mv. Always choose a consistent sign convention and SI units.
**Worked example:** A 2 kg object accelerating at 3 m/s² has resultant force F=2×3=6 N.

## Quick Revision Checklist
- Distinguish permutations from combinations.
- Practise complex-number operations and matrix determinants.
- Memorise and apply differentiation and integration rules.
- Interpret gradients, stationary points and definite integrals.
- Use probability formulas only when their assumptions fit.
- Show full working, units and sensible rounding in mechanics problems.
