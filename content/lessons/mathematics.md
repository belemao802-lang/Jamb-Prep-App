# Mathematics — Topic-by-Topic Lessons
**Content type:** Original learning material. **Status:** Initial teaching pack; compare the topic list and learning objectives with the current official JAMB Mathematics syllabus before claiming complete coverage.

## 1. Number Bases
A base tells how many digits are used before carrying to the next place. Decimal is base 10; binary is base 2. In base (b), place values are powers of (b). For example, (231_4=2(4^2)+3(4)+1=45_{10}). To convert a positive decimal integer to another base, divide repeatedly by the target base and read the remainders upwards. Addition and multiplication in a base follow the same place-value principle, but carrying occurs when a column reaches the base.
**Subtopics:** place value; conversion between bases; addition, subtraction and multiplication; applications to number representation.
**Check:** Convert (1011_2) to decimal: (1(8)+0(4)+1(2)+1=11).

## 2. Fractions, Decimals and Percentages
A fraction represents a ratio of integers with a non-zero denominator. To add or subtract fractions, use a common denominator; multiply numerators and denominators directly; divide by multiplying by the reciprocal. A percentage is a fraction out of 100: (p\%=p/100). Percentage change is ((\text{change}/\text{original})\times100\%). Repeated percentage changes are applied successively, not simply added.
**Subtopics:** equivalent fractions; four operations; recurring decimals; conversion among fractions, decimals and percentages; percentage increase/decrease; profit, loss, discount and simple applications.
**Worked example:** 15% of 240 is (0.15\times240=36).

## 3. Indices and Logarithms
For non-zero (a), (a^m a^n=a^{m+n}), (a^m/a^n=a^{m-n}), and ((a^m)^n=a^{mn}). Also (a^0=1), (a^{-n}=1/a^n), and (a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}) where defined. A logarithm reverses exponentiation: (log_a x=y) means (a^y=x), with (a>0), (a\ne1), and (x>0). The laws include (log_a(xy)=log_a x+log_a y) and (log_a(x/y)=log_a x-log_a y).
**Subtopics:** laws of indices; negative and fractional powers; standard form; definition and laws of logarithms; solving exponential/logarithmic equations.
**Worked example:** (2^x=32=2^5), so (x=5).

## 4. Sets and Venn Diagrams
A set is a well-defined collection. (A\cup B) contains elements in either set; (A\cap B) contains elements common to both; (A'\) contains elements in the universal set but not in (A). For two finite sets, (n(A\cup B)=n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B)). The intersection is subtracted once because it was counted twice.
**Subtopics:** notation and membership; subsets; universal and empty sets; union and intersection; complements; Venn diagrams; counting problems.
**Worked example:** If 18 study Biology, 15 study Chemistry, and 6 study both, then (n(B\cup C)=18+15-6=27).

## 5. Ratio, Proportion and Rates
A ratio compares quantities in the same units. Simplify a ratio by dividing each term by a common factor. In direct proportion, (y=kx); in inverse proportion, (y=k/x), where (k) is constant. Rates compare different quantities, such as distance per time or cost per item. Convert units before comparing or calculating.
**Subtopics:** simplifying ratios; sharing in a ratio; direct and inverse proportion; rates; speed, distance and time; scale and unit conversion.
**Worked example:** Share ₦1,200 in ratio 2:3. There are 5 parts; each part is ₦240, so the shares are ₦480 and ₦720.

## 6. Approximation and Bounds
Rounding replaces a number with a nearby value at a stated accuracy. Significant figures begin at the first non-zero digit; decimal places count digits after the decimal point. If a value rounds to 3.4 to the nearest 0.1, its lower bound is 3.35 and its upper bound is 3.45, using the usual lower-inclusive, upper-exclusive convention. For measurements given to a stated precision, bounds describe possible true values.
**Subtopics:** decimal places; significant figures; rounding; estimation; upper and lower bounds; error and percentage error.
**Worked example:** 7.846 to 2 decimal places is 7.85.

## 7. Algebraic Expressions
An algebraic expression combines variables, constants and operations. Like terms have the same variable parts and powers: (3x+5x=8x), but (3x+5x^2) cannot be combined. Expand brackets using the distributive law: (a(b+c)=ab+ac). Factorisation reverses expansion and may use a common factor, difference of squares, or quadratic factorisation.
**Subtopics:** terms and coefficients; collecting like terms; expansion; common-factor factorisation; difference of two squares; algebraic fractions and substitution.
**Worked example:** (3(x+4)-2x=3x+12-2x=x+12).

## 8. Linear Equations and Inequalities
An equation remains balanced when the same operation is applied to both sides. Solve by simplifying, collecting variable terms on one side and constants on the other, then dividing by the coefficient. For inequalities, multiplying or dividing by a negative number reverses the inequality sign. Simultaneous linear equations can be solved by substitution or elimination.
**Subtopics:** one-variable equations; brackets and fractions; inequalities and number lines; simultaneous equations; word problems.
**Worked example:** (3x-7=11\Rightarrow3x=18\Rightarrow x=6).

## 9. Quadratic Equations
A quadratic has form (ax^2+bx+c=0), (a\ne0). Solve by factorisation, completing the square, or the quadratic formula (x=(-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac})/(2a)). The discriminant (D=b^2-4ac) tells the nature of roots: (D>0) gives two distinct real roots, (D=0) gives equal real roots, and (D<0) gives no real roots.
**Subtopics:** factorisation; completing the square; quadratic formula; discriminant; forming equations; quadratic word problems.
**Worked example:** (x^2-5x+6=0\Rightarrow(x-2)(x-3)=0), so (x=2) or (3).

## 10. Sequences and Series
A sequence is an ordered list; a series is the sum of its terms. In an arithmetic progression (AP), the common difference is (d), and (T_n=a+(n-1)d). Its first (n) terms sum to (S_n=n(a+l)/2), where (l) is the last term. In a geometric progression (GP), the common ratio is (r), and (T_n=ar^{n-1}). For (r\ne1), (S_n=a(r^n-1)/(r-1)).
**Subtopics:** patterns; nth term of AP and GP; finite sums; arithmetic means; geometric means; applications.
**Worked example:** AP 4, 7, 10,… has (a=4,d=3); its 10th term is (4+9(3)=31).

## 11. Variation
Variation describes how one quantity depends on another. Direct variation (y\propto x) means (y=kx); inverse variation (y\propto1/x) means (y=k/x). Joint variation may be (y=kxz), while combined variation may involve both direct and inverse factors. Find the constant (k) from given values before using the relationship to calculate unknowns.
**Subtopics:** direct, inverse, joint and combined variation; powers in variation; determining constants; applied problems.
**Worked example:** If (y) varies directly as (x), and (y=12) when (x=3), then (k=4); when (x=5), (y=20).

## 12. Functions and Graphs
A function assigns exactly one output to each permitted input. (f(x)) means the value of the function at input (x). The domain is the set of allowed inputs; the range is the set of outputs. For a straight line (y=mx+c), (m) is the gradient and (c) is the vertical intercept. Graphs help solve equations, identify intersections and interpret trends.
**Subtopics:** function notation; domain and range; linear and quadratic graphs; gradient and intercepts; plotting tables; solving equations graphically; interpretation.
**Worked example:** If (f(x)=2x+1), then (f(3)=7).

## 13. Coordinate Geometry
Points are represented by coordinates ((x,y)). The gradient between ((x_1,y_1)) and ((x_2,y_2)) is (m=(y_2-y_1)/(x_2-x_1)), where (x_2\ne x_1). The midpoint is (((x_1+x_2)/2,(y_1+y_2)/2)), and the distance is (\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}). A line can be written (y=mx+c).
**Subtopics:** Cartesian coordinates; distance and midpoint; gradient; equations of straight lines; parallel and perpendicular lines; intersections.
**Worked example:** The gradient from (1,2) to (3,8) is ((8-2)/(3-1)=3).

## 14. Plane Geometry and Mensuration
Geometry studies shapes and their properties. Triangle angles sum to 180°; angles on a straight line sum to 180°; angles around a point sum to 360°. Mensuration applies formulas for perimeter, area, surface area and volume. Keep units consistent and square or cube them for area or volume.
**Subtopics:** angle properties; polygons; congruence and similarity; perimeter; area of triangles, quadrilaterals and circles; surface area and volume of solids; scale drawings.
**Key formulas:** rectangle area (=lw); triangle area (=\frac12 bh); circle area (=\pi r^2); cylinder volume (=\pi r^2h).

## 15. Circle Geometry
A circle is the set of points at a fixed distance from its centre. A radius joins centre to circumference; a diameter is twice the radius. Important results include: angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference standing on the same arc; angles in the same segment are equal; the angle in a semicircle is 90°; opposite angles in a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°; a tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
**Subtopics:** chords and arcs; angles in a circle; cyclic quadrilaterals; tangents; intersecting chords and secants; circle theorems.
**Study tip:** Draw and label the circle before applying a theorem; identify which arc or chord the angles stand on.

## 16. Trigonometry
For a right triangle and acute angle \(\theta\), \(\sin\theta=\text{opposite}/\text{hypotenuse}\), \(\cos\theta=\text{adjacent}/\text{hypotenuse}\), and \(\tan\theta=\text{opposite}/\text{adjacent}\). The identity \(\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1\) is useful for checking results. Sine rule and cosine rule extend calculations to non-right triangles.
**Subtopics:** trigonometric ratios; special angles; bearings and elevation/depression; sine rule; cosine rule; area of a triangle; trigonometric graphs and identities.
**Worked example:** In a right triangle, if opposite = 3 and hypotenuse = 5, then \(\sin\theta=3/5\).

## 17. Vectors
A vector has magnitude and direction; a scalar has magnitude only. In two dimensions, vectors can be represented as columns or components. Add vectors by adding corresponding components; multiply by a scalar by multiplying each component. Magnitude of \((a,b)\) is \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). Displacement and velocity are vector quantities.
**Subtopics:** scalar and vector quantities; representation; addition and subtraction; scalar multiplication; magnitude; position vectors; vector geometry.
**Worked example:** (2,3)+(4,-1)=(6,2).

## 18. Matrices and Determinants
A matrix is a rectangular arrangement of numbers. Its order is rows × columns. Matrices of the same order can be added or subtracted entry by entry; matrix multiplication requires the number of columns of the first matrix to equal the number of rows of the second. For a 2×2 matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\), determinant is \(ad-bc\). If the determinant is non-zero, its inverse exists.
**Subtopics:** order and types; equality; addition and subtraction; multiplication; determinants; inverse of 2×2 matrices; solving simultaneous equations by matrices.
**Worked example:** determinant of [[2,1],[3,4]] is (2(4)-1(3)=5).

## 19. Permutation and Combination
A permutation counts arrangements where order matters; a combination counts selections where order does not matter. (n!=n(n-1)\cdots1), with (0!=1). The number of arrangements of (r) objects selected from (n) distinct objects is \(^{n}P_r=n!/(n-r)!\); the number of selections is \(^{n}C_r=n!/[r!(n-r)!]\).
**Subtopics:** factorial notation; arrangements; selections; repeated restrictions; counting principles; practical word problems.
**Worked example:** Selecting 2 students from 5 is \(^{5}C_2=10\).

## 20. Probability
Probability measures the chance of an event. For equally likely outcomes, (P(A)=n(A)/n(S)), where (S) is the sample space. Probability lies between 0 and 1. The complement rule is (P(A')=1-P(A)). For independent events, (P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)); for mutually exclusive events, (P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)). Do not assume events are independent unless stated or justified.
**Subtopics:** sample spaces; events; equally likely outcomes; complements; mutually exclusive and independent events; combined events; tree diagrams.
**Worked example:** A fair die has three even faces out of six, so (P(\text{even})=3/6=1/2).

## 21. Statistics and Data Representation
Statistics involves collecting, organising, summarising and interpreting data. Mean is total divided by number of observations; median is the middle value after ordering; mode is the most frequent value. For grouped data, class midpoints and frequencies are used for estimates. Range is maximum minus minimum. Graphs should have labelled axes, appropriate scales and clear units.
**Subtopics:** frequency tables; bar charts and histograms; pie charts; mean, median and mode; range; cumulative frequency; quartiles and interpretation.
**Worked example:** For 2, 4, 4, 8, the mean is 18/4=4.5, median is 4 and mode is 4.

## 22. Differentiation
Differentiation finds a function's instantaneous rate of change or gradient. The power rule is \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^n)=nx^{n-1}\). Constants differentiate to zero, and sums can be differentiated term by term. A stationary point occurs where the derivative is zero; its nature may be investigated using the graph or second derivative where appropriate.
**Subtopics:** gradient as a limit/instantaneous rate; power rule; derivatives of polynomials; tangent and normal; stationary points; increasing/decreasing behaviour; simple rate-of-change problems.
**Worked example:** If (y=3x^2+2x-4), then (dy/dx=6x+2).

## 23. Integration
Integration reverses differentiation for finding antiderivatives and can calculate areas. The power rule is \(\int x^n dx=x^{n+1}/(n+1)+C\) for (n\ne-1). The constant (C) is included for indefinite integrals. A definite integral evaluates an antiderivative at the upper limit minus its value at the lower limit. Interpret area carefully when a graph lies below the axis.
**Subtopics:** indefinite integrals; power rule; constant of integration; definite integrals; area under simple curves; checking by differentiation.
**Worked example:** \(\int 4x^3 dx=x^4+C\).
